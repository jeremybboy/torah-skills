Welcome to a short Torah Skills preview of Toldot. Here is our question: what does it mean to inherit a blessing, and what does it take to become responsible for it?

Genesis introduces Isaac and Rebecca, who wait for children. Isaac prays, and Rebecca conceives twins. Before their birth, Rebecca hears that the children represent two peoples and that the older will serve the younger. The story tells us what she hears, but it does not say here that she shares it with Isaac. As you listen, notice who knows what, and when.

When the brothers are older, Esau returns hungry and asks Jacob for food. Jacob asks for the birthright in exchange. Later, Rebecca helps Jacob present himself to Isaac as Esau to receive a blessing. These are two different scenes. The first is a bargain between brothers; the second is a deception in front of their father. Genesis gives us room to ask what each character wanted without asking us to excuse every means they used.

Between those scenes, Isaac faces a different kind of struggle. He reopens wells dug in Abraham's time, and his servants dig wells of their own. At the first two wells there is conflict. He moves on and digs again. At the third, there is no dispute. Isaac names it Rehovot, a name that recalls the room God has made for them. In the plain story, Isaac does not stop looking for water. He changes where he digs. In a distinct medieval interpretation, Ramban reads the wells as pointing beyond Isaac's lifetime, to the Temples. That reading adds a historical horizon; it does not replace the plain story of people contending over water.

Return now to Isaac's tent. Isaac recognizes Jacob's voice, but the hands feel like Esau's. One clue points one way; another points the other way. The story lets us sit with that mismatch. After the deception comes to light, Isaac does not retract the blessing. Later, before Jacob leaves, Isaac knowingly gives him the blessing of Abraham. Listen for the difference between what Jacob obtains in disguise and what Isaac gives him openly.

For your own study, choose one moment: Rebecca hearing the oracle, Isaac digging another well, or Jacob standing before his father. Ask first what the text tells you. Then ask what you are supplying yourself. Toldot leaves us with a question worth keeping open: when we believe something important is at stake, what do we owe the people who stand in our way?
