This week we’re reading Vayishlach, in the book of Genesis. Jacob is returning to Canaan with his family. Years earlier, he fled from his twin brother Esau after receiving the blessing their father Isaac meant to give Esau. Now Jacob hears that his brother is coming to meet him with four hundred men.

Jacob is afraid. He divides his camp so that if one group is attacked, the other might escape. Then he prays and sends a gift ahead.

God had promised to bring him back to this land. So if Jacob trusts that promise, why make a plan for what might go wrong?

[pause 3.0]

In Genesis, chapter thirty-two, Jacob says, “Save me, please, from the hand of my brother, from the hand of Esau.” In the same prayer, he recalls God’s promise. Rashi sees three preparations here. Jacob offers a gift, prays, and gets ready to defend himself.

Each action has a purpose. Dividing the camp protects his family. The gift is a gesture of peace. In prayer, Jacob asks for what his plans alone can’t guarantee. He takes responsibility for what he can do and asks for help with the outcome.

[pause 1.1]

That night, Jacob wrestles with a stranger and receives the name Israel. The next day, Esau runs to meet him. He embraces him, kisses him, and they weep. Rashi gives two readings of the kiss. One questions its sincerity. The other sees genuine compassion in that moment. The brothers go their separate ways.

How much can we learn from that embrace? It gives Jacob a reason to reconsider what he expected, and it leaves the future of the relationship open. Before a difficult conversation of your own, ask two questions. What can I do to prepare? What might I hear that would change my mind?

This Shabbat’s haftarah, Obadiah, takes the question of brotherhood from a family to two peoples. The prophet holds Edom to account for violence against its brother Jacob. Read alongside the reunion, it leaves us with a question. What do we owe each other when there’s a long history between us?
