# Torah Skills Weekly Study

## Parashat Vayera

**Draft status:** AI-assisted study draft for human review before publication.

**Reading:** [Genesis 18:1-22:24](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.18.1-22.24).

**Date and place:** Shabbat, 20 Cheshvan 5787 / October 31, 2026, Los Angeles.

**Haftarah:** Ashkenazi communities read [II Kings 4:1-37](https://www.sefaria.org/II_Kings.4.1-37); Sephardi communities commonly conclude at [II Kings 4:23](https://www.sefaria.org/II_Kings.4.1-23).

**Core theme:** Vayera tests whether encounter with God produces responsibility toward the guest, the endangered city, the excluded child, and the beloved child—and whether faith can recognize the moral force of a divine interruption.

---

## 0. Orientation and Familiar Hook

Vayera is the fourth weekly reading in Genesis. Lech-Lecha ended with circumcision and the promise that Sarah would bear Isaac; Vayera opens while Abraham is recovering, seated at the entrance of his tent in the heat of the day. The narrative then moves through hospitality, the announcement of Isaac's birth, Abraham's argument over Sodom, the destruction of the cities, Lot's rescue, the births and expulsions that shape Abraham's household, and the binding of Isaac.

The familiar Jewish-life hook is *hachnasat orchim*, welcoming guests. Abraham's hurried service becomes a classical model of hospitality, but the parashah will not allow that virtue to remain private. Sodom shows what happens when cruelty becomes social structure, while Abraham's intercession asks whether the Judge of all the earth must also be answerable to justice.

The ending creates a second familiar hook. The ram of the Akedah enters the memory of Rosh Hashanah, while the divine command to stop Abraham's hand remains the indispensable boundary inside the story. The test is not permission to harm in God's name.

---

## 1. Opening Reflection

**Framing question:** What does it mean to encounter God if the encounter does not make us more attentive to the vulnerable person standing nearby?

**Reflection prompts**

1. Why does Abraham interrupt a divine encounter to serve strangers?
2. What gives Abraham the courage to argue for justice without claiming moral superiority?
3. How can laughter express disbelief, relief, fear, and joy at different moments?
4. What does it mean for a person to be judged “where he is” rather than by a feared future?
5. Where does the Akedah establish a boundary against turning devotion into violence?

**Central theme:** Vayera joins revelation to moral attention: seeing God must sharpen the ability to see the guest, challenge institutional cruelty, hear the endangered child, and stop when life is at risk.

---

## 2. Narrative Overview

**Main people and relationships:** Abraham and Sarah are the covenantal couple awaiting Isaac; the three visitors announce his birth; Lot is Abraham's nephew living in Sodom; Hagar is Sarah's Egyptian servant and Ishmael's mother; Ishmael and Isaac are Abraham's sons; Abimelech is king of Gerar.

**Literary setting:** The opening word, *vayera*—“He appeared”—announces divine revelation, yet the story immediately focuses on Abraham seeing three people. That verbal movement from God's appearance to human sight frames the parashah: revelation is tested by what Abraham notices and does.

**Chronological flow**

1. Abraham welcomes three visitors, who announce that Sarah will bear a son.
2. Sarah laughs at the promise, and God asks whether anything is too wondrous.
3. God discloses the judgment of Sodom; Abraham argues that the righteous must not be swept away with the wicked.
4. The visitors reach Sodom; Lot shelters them, the city's violence erupts, and Lot's family escapes as the cities are destroyed.
5. Abraham and Sarah travel to Gerar, where the sister-wife strategy again places Sarah in a king's household before divine intervention.
6. Isaac is born and named for laughter.
7. Sarah demands the expulsion of Hagar and Ishmael; Abraham is distressed, and God hears Ishmael in the wilderness.
8. Abraham and Abimelech establish a covenant at Beer-sheba.
9. God tests Abraham by commanding him to offer Isaac; an angel stops his hand, and a ram is offered instead.

**Repetition and comparison:** Hospitality appears twice, first in Abraham's open tent and then in Lot's besieged house. Endangered sons also appear twice: Ishmael in the wilderness and Isaac on the mountain. The parallels do not erase their differences; they force the reader to hear divine concern for both children.

**One-sentence summary:** Vayera turns revelation into a series of moral tests about welcome, justice, exclusion, promise, and the boundary that prevents devotion from consuming life.

## 3. Five Principal Sugyot

### Sugya 1: Hospitality in the Presence of God

**Source:** [Genesis 18:1-8](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.18.1-8); [Rashi on Genesis 18:1](https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Genesis.18.1); [Bava Metzia 86b](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Metzia.86b); [Shabbat 127a:13](https://www.sefaria.org/Shabbat.127a.13); [Mishneh Torah, Mourning 14:1-2](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishneh_Torah%2C_Mourning.14.1-2).

**Source categories:** Torah; Rashi is a medieval commentator drawing here on Midrash and Gemara; Bava Metzia and Shabbat are Gemara; Mishneh Torah is Halachah.

**Context:** Abraham sits at the tent entrance after circumcision. When three people appear, he runs, bows, offers modest refreshments, and prepares an abundant meal.

**Kushya:** Why does the narrative move from God's appearance to Abraham's urgent care for unidentified travelers?

**Classical responses / terutz:** Rashi, drawing on the rabbinic tradition developed in Bava Metzia 86b, reads God's appearance as visiting the sick and the visitors as an opportunity created when Abraham longs for guests. Shabbat 127a formulates the stronger claim that welcoming guests is greater than receiving the Divine Presence. The conclusion is not that God is displaced by human need; rather, service to the guest becomes the concrete response to divine encounter.

**Halachic implications:** Rambam includes hospitality and escorting guests among acts of lovingkindness, stressing that escorting a guest carries exceptional weight. The legal development converts Abraham's personal generosity into a communal duty rather than leaving it as heroic temperament.

**Philosophical implications:** Spiritual attention is falsified when it narrows perception. Abraham's seeing of God is credible because it expands his sight toward people whose identity and usefulness are still unknown.

**Personal implications and application:** Build hospitality around the guest's dignity rather than the host's performance. Notice, invite, feed, and accompany without making the recipient repay care with gratitude or agreement.

**Core idea:** Encounter with God becomes morally legible when it opens the door to another person.

### Sugya 2: Abraham's Argument and Sodom's Institutional Cruelty

**Source:** [Genesis 18:16-33](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.18.16-33); [Genesis 18:19](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.18.19); [Rashi on Genesis 18:25](https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Genesis.18.25); [Ezekiel 16:49](https://www.sefaria.org/Ezekiel.16.49); [Sanhedrin 109a-109b](https://www.sefaria.org/Sanhedrin.109a-109b).

**Source categories:** Torah; Rashi is a Rishon; Ezekiel is Prophets; Sanhedrin is Gemara whose material here is substantially aggadic.

**Context:** God links Abraham's election to teaching his household “righteousness and justice,” then reveals the outcry against Sodom. Abraham approaches and repeatedly asks whether a righteous remnant can save the city.

**Kushya:** Is Abraham bargaining against divine judgment, defending universal justice, or learning the responsibility implied by covenant?

**Classical responses / terutz:** Rashi reads Abraham's protest as a demand for true judgment and a warning that destroying righteous and wicked together would profane the Judge's reputation. Ezekiel later identifies Sodom's sin with pride, abundance, complacency, and failure to strengthen the poor. Sanhedrin 109a-b intensifies that critique through aggadic portraits of cruelty embedded in law and custom; those stories are rabbinic moral interpretation, not a historical report of municipal statutes.

**Halachic implications:** Abraham's exchange is not a procedural court code, but it establishes a norm that power must distinguish cases and answer the cry of victims. Legal authority without justice is precisely the institutional failure attributed to Sodom.

**Philosophical implications:** Abraham does not possess all the facts, yet he refuses moral passivity. Reverence permits argument when the character of justice is at stake.

**Personal implications and application:** Ask whether an institution's ordinary rules systematically punish need, difference, or dependence. Private charity cannot compensate for a system designed to manufacture exclusion.

**Core idea:** Covenant trains Abraham not merely to obey power, but to demand that power remain accountable to justice.

### Sugya 3: Laughter, Promise, and the Impossible Birth

**Source:** [Genesis 18:9-15](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.18.9-15); [Genesis 21:1-7](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.21.1-7); [Rashi on Genesis 18:13](https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Genesis.18.13); [Bava Metzia 87a](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Metzia.87a); [II Kings 4:8-37](https://www.sefaria.org/II_Kings.4.8-37).

**Source categories:** Torah; Rashi is a Rishon drawing on Gemara; Bava Metzia is Gemara; II Kings is Prophets and the haftarah source.

**Context:** Sarah hears that she will bear a son and laughs inwardly. After Isaac's birth, she says that God has made laughter for her and that everyone who hears will laugh with her.

**Kushya:** Why does the Torah preserve Sarah's skeptical laughter and then make laughter the promised child's name?

**Classical responses / terutz:** Rashi and Bava Metzia 87a notice that God reports Sarah's words to Abraham without repeating her reference to his age; the sages derive that wording may be altered for peace. That rabbinic inference is not the verse's only peshat, but it identifies relational care inside divine rebuke. The narrative itself transforms private, anxious laughter into public, shared laughter without pretending the first response never occurred.

**Haftarah connection:** The Shunammite woman also practices hospitality and receives an improbable promise of a son. The later threat to that child's life gives the haftarah an additional echo of the Akedah, while the differing Ashkenazi and Sephardi endpoints change how fully that danger and restoration are heard.

**Philosophical implications:** Wonder does not demand emotional purity. Promise can meet disbelief, and fulfillment can reinterpret an earlier reaction without humiliating the person who doubted.

**Personal implications and application:** Treat another person's guarded response to hope as evidence of history, not moral failure. Trust grows when promise becomes dependable reality.

**Core idea:** Isaac's name turns laughter from a record of impossibility into a shared memory of possibility.

### Sugya 4: Hagar, Ishmael, and Judgment “Where He Is”

**Source:** [Genesis 21:9-21](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.21.9-21); [Rashi on Genesis 21:17](https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Genesis.21.17); [Rosh Hashanah 16b](https://www.sefaria.org/Rosh_Hashanah.16b).

**Source categories:** Torah; Rashi is a Rishon citing Midrash; Rosh Hashanah is Gemara.

**Context:** Sarah sees Ishmael *metzachek*, a deliberately ambiguous word related to laughter, and demands that Hagar and Ishmael be expelled. Abraham is distressed; God tells him to heed Sarah concerning Isaac's covenantal line, while also promising a nation from Ishmael.

**Kushya:** How can the chapter celebrate Isaac's laughter while another reading of laughter becomes the occasion for Ishmael's expulsion?

**Classical responses / terutz:** Traditions about *metzachek* range from ordinary play to grave misconduct, so the uncertainty must remain explicit. Rashi, drawing on the Midrash reflected in Rosh Hashanah 16b, reads God's hearing Ishmael “where he is” as judgment according to present deeds rather than a feared future. The teaching constrains anticipatory condemnation, but it does not remove the narrative's human suffering or resolve every moral question about the expulsion.

**Halachic implications:** The phrase “where he is” becomes a principle about present judgment, not a complete legal doctrine for predicting danger. It warns decision-makers against treating imagined future wrongdoing as established guilt.

**Philosophical implications:** Divine hearing extends beyond the covenantal line centered on Isaac. Election does not make another endangered child inaudible.

**Personal implications and application:** When fear projects a future identity onto a child, return to the person actually present. Protection must be evidence-based and proportionate, not a license for abandonment.

**Core idea:** God hears the endangered child as he is now, before others' predictions define him.

### Sugya 5: The Akedah—Test, Boundary, and Substitution

**Source:** [Genesis 22:1-19](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.22.1-19); [Ramban on Genesis 22:1](https://www.sefaria.org/Ramban_on_Genesis.22.1); [Rosh Hashanah 16a](https://www.sefaria.org/Rosh_Hashanah.16a).

**Source categories:** Torah; Ramban is a Rishon; Rosh Hashanah is Gemara and provides an aggadic rationale for later ritual memory.

**Context:** God explicitly tests Abraham by commanding him to take Isaac to the land of Moriah. At the decisive moment, an angel commands Abraham not to send his hand against the boy; a ram is offered instead.

**Kushya:** If God knows Abraham, what can the test reveal, and why must the story end with a prohibition and substitution?

**Classical responses / terutz:** Ramban explains that a test brings human potential into enacted reality and allows reward for a deed rather than only an intention. Rosh Hashanah 16a connects the ram's horn with remembrance of the binding of Isaac. Neither source cancels the narrative's hard stop: the command “do not send your hand” and the substituted ram belong to the meaning of the test, not to an optional epilogue.

**Halachic implications:** The Akedah shapes memory and liturgy, but it does not authorize private revelation to override the prohibition against harming a child. The story cannot be responsibly invoked to sanctify abuse, coercion, or reckless sacrifice.

**Philosophical implications:** Faith reaches a boundary where obedience must not become possession of another person's life. The enduring symbol is not only the raised knife but the stopped hand and the life that continues.

**Personal implications and application:** Identify the person or value your mission is tempted to consume. Build a hard stop before devotion becomes destruction.

**Core idea:** The Akedah's moral memory includes the test, the interruption, and the substitution that preserves Isaac's life.

## 4. Five Key Verses

### Verse 1 - Genesis 18:2

**Hebrew:** וישא עיניו וירא והנה שלשה אנשים נצבים עליו וירא וירץ לקראתם מפתח האהל וישתחו ארצה

**Transliteration:** Vayisa einav vayar, vehineh sheloshah anashim nitzavim alav; vayar vayaratz likratam mipetach ha'ohel, vayishtachu artzah.

**Translation:** He lifted his eyes and saw: three people were standing near him. He saw, ran to meet them from the tent entrance, and bowed toward the ground.

**Peshat:** Abraham sees travelers, moves urgently toward them, and receives them with deference before knowing their mission.

**Commentary:** Shabbat 127a reads the episode as evidence that welcoming guests can take precedence even over receiving the Divine Presence.

**Why it matters:** Revelation expands Abraham's attention instead of enclosing him in a private spiritual experience.

### Verse 2 - Genesis 18:19

**Hebrew:** כי ידעתיו למען אשר יצוה את בניו ואת ביתו אחריו ושמרו דרך יהוה לעשות צדקה ומשפט למען הביא יהוה על אברהם את אשר דבר עליו

**Transliteration:** Ki yedativ lema'an asher yetzaveh et banav ve'et beito acharav, veshamru derech Adonai la'asot tzedakah u-mishpat, lema'an havi Adonai al Avraham et asher diber alav.

**Translation:** For I have known him so that he may command his children and his household after him to keep the way of the Lord by doing righteousness and justice, so that the Lord may bring upon Abraham what was spoken concerning him.

**Peshat:** Abraham's covenantal role includes transmitting a practiced way of righteousness and justice across his household.

**Commentary:** The verse explains why God includes Abraham in the deliberation about Sodom: election creates responsibility for justice rather than exemption from scrutiny.

**Why it matters:** A tradition survives not by inherited identity alone, but by teaching the next generation how power must serve justice.

### Verse 3 - Genesis 18:25

**Hebrew:** חללה לך מעשת כדבר הזה להמית צדיק עם רשע והיה כצדיק כרשע חללה לך השפט כל הארץ לא יעשה משפט

**Transliteration:** Chalilah lecha me'asot kadavar hazeh, lehamit tzaddik im rasha, vehayah chatzaddik karasha; chalilah lach—hashofet kol ha'aretz lo ya'aseh mishpat?

**Translation:** Far be it from You to do such a thing—to kill the righteous with the wicked, making righteous and wicked alike. Far be it from You! Shall the Judge of all the earth not do justice?

**Peshat:** Abraham argues that indiscriminate destruction would contradict the justice expected of the world's Judge.

**Commentary:** Rashi emphasizes both accurate judgment and the profanation that would result if divine justice appeared morally arbitrary.

**Why it matters:** Reverence does not silence moral argument; it gives Abraham the ground from which to make it.

### Verse 4 - Genesis 21:17

**Hebrew:** וישמע אלהים את קול הנער ויקרא מלאך אלהים אל הגר מן השמים ויאמר לה מה לך הגר אל תיראי כי שמע אלהים אל קול הנער באשר הוא שם

**Transliteration:** Vayishma Elohim et kol hana'ar, vayikra malach Elohim el Hagar min hashamayim, vayomer lah: mah lach Hagar, al tiri, ki shama Elohim el kol hana'ar ba'asher hu sham.

**Translation:** God heard the boy's voice, and an angel of God called to Hagar from heaven and said to her: What troubles you, Hagar? Do not fear, for God has heard the boy's voice where he is.

**Peshat:** God hears the endangered child and directs Hagar toward the water that will sustain them.

**Commentary:** Rashi and Rosh Hashanah 16b read “where he is” as present-tense judgment rather than punishment for a predicted future.

**Why it matters:** A feared future must not erase the moral reality of a child in immediate need.

### Verse 5 - Genesis 22:12

**Hebrew:** ויאמר אל תשלח ידך אל הנער ואל תעש לו מאומה כי עתה ידעתי כי ירא אלהים אתה ולא חשכת את בנך את יחידך ממני

**Transliteration:** Vayomer: al tishlach yadcha el hana'ar, ve'al ta'as lo me'umah; ki atah yadati ki yere Elohim atah, velo chasachta et bincha et yechidcha mimeni.

**Translation:** He said: Do not send your hand against the boy, and do nothing to him; for now I know that you are one who fears God, since you have not withheld your son, your only one, from Me.

**Peshat:** The angel stops Abraham before Isaac is harmed and marks the test as complete.

**Commentary:** Ramban understands the test as bringing potential faithfulness into action; the stopping command defines the action's boundary.

**Why it matters:** Any reading of the Akedah that omits “do nothing to him” omits the verse that preserves Isaac's life.

## 5. Advanced Interpretation

### A. Mishnah, Talmud, and Midrash

The rabbinic sources make Vayera an ethical training ground. [Shabbat 127a](https://www.sefaria.org/Shabbat.127a.13) elevates hospitality; [Bava Metzia 87a](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Metzia.87a) reads God's altered report of Sarah's speech as concern for peace; [Sanhedrin 109a-b](https://www.sefaria.org/Sanhedrin.109a-109b) depicts Sodom's cruelty as institutional; and [Rosh Hashanah 16b](https://www.sefaria.org/Rosh_Hashanah.16b) insists that Ishmael is judged in his present state. These are Gemara and Aggadah, not the Torah's peshat, but together they show the sages testing theology by its effects on human dignity.

### B. Rishonim

Rashi makes the chapter's moral details immediate: God visits the sick, Abraham's protest protects the integrity of justice, God preserves peace in reporting Sarah's words, and Ishmael is heard in the present. Ramban's reading of [Genesis 22:1](https://www.sefaria.org/Ramban_on_Genesis.22.1) explains divine testing as the movement from capacity to deed. Their methods differ, yet both refuse a faith that remains abstract: virtue must become action and action remains subject to moral interpretation.

### C. Kabbalah and Chassidut

This layer must remain distinct from peshat. [Zohar, Vayera](https://www.sefaria.org/Zohar%2C_Vayera.2.19-27.422) magnifies the spiritual merit of Abraham's hospitality and escort even when the guests are angels. A later Chassidic work, [Me'or Einayim on Vayera](https://www.sefaria.org/Me'or_Einayim%2C_Vayera), turns the parashah inward toward awareness of divine presence. These readings can deepen practice, but they must not overwrite the narrative's vulnerable bodies or replace its ethical claims with symbolism.

### D. Halachic Development

[Mishneh Torah, Mourning 14:1-2](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishneh_Torah%2C_Mourning.14.1-2) places hospitality and escort among concrete duties of lovingkindness. The Akedah's ram also enters Rosh Hashanah memory through [Rosh Hashanah 16a](https://www.sefaria.org/Rosh_Hashanah.16a), but that aggadic explanation is not itself the biblical command to sound the shofar. The distinction matters: narrative, ritual memory, and binding legal practice are connected categories, not interchangeable ones.

## 6. Personal Application

**Practical**

1. Design one act of hospitality around what the guest needs rather than what displays the host.
2. Audit one rule in your household or organization for who is excluded by its ordinary operation.
3. When fear predicts another person's future, separate evidence from projection.
4. Build a non-negotiable stop condition into any demanding commitment.
5. Convert a private value into one visible act of righteousness and justice.

**Emotional**

Vayera does not demand a single clean emotional response. Sarah laughs, Abraham argues, Hagar fears, Abraham grieves, and Isaac walks into silence. The chapter's discipline is not emotional suppression but responsible action amid conflicting feelings.

**Spiritual**

Divine encounter is not measured by intensity alone. Its evidence is increased attention: to the visitor, the outcry, the mother, the child, and the command to stop.

**Reflective question:** Which person becomes more visible because of the way you practice faith—and who is still being pushed outside the frame?

## 7. Parenting Reflection

Vayera places adults' convictions next to children's vulnerability. Isaac carries the family's promise; Ishmael carries a different divine promise; both become endangered through decisions made by adults. A covenantal family therefore cannot treat children as instruments of destiny, proof of faith, or containers for adult fear.

The educational implication is direct. Genesis 18:19 defines transmission as teaching children to do righteousness and justice, while Genesis 22:12 establishes the boundary “do nothing to him.” Parents form moral heirs not by demanding unquestioning imitation, but by modeling welcome, accountable power, truthful argument, and protection of life.

**Practice:** Ask a child what makes a rule feel fair, then listen for evidence you did not have when the rule was made. Revision can be a form of authority exercised responsibly.

## 8. Dialectical Analysis

**Thesis:** Abraham embodies radical responsiveness. He runs to guests, intercedes for a city, sends Hagar away when told to heed Sarah, and rises early for the test of Isaac.

**Antithesis:** Responsiveness without moral differentiation can endanger the vulnerable. Hospitality does not erase Abraham's repeated sister-wife strategy; covenantal promise does not erase Hagar and Ishmael's suffering; willingness to sacrifice does not erase Isaac's life.

**Synthesis:** Vayera defines faithful action through both movement and interruption. Abraham must act, but the text also preserves protest, grief, divine hearing, and the final command to stop. Mature covenantal agency is therefore responsive without becoming morally numb.

**Deeper tension:** Abraham argues against collective destruction in Sodom but does not verbally argue for Isaac in the recorded Akedah narrative. The contrast is real and unresolved; responsible study should not invent a single answer that the text withholds.

**Systems view:** Hospitality, law, family decisions, and ritual memory form one moral ecology. Private virtue is insufficient if institutions encode cruelty, and institutional justice is incomplete if a child at the margin remains unheard.

---

## 9. Cross-Disciplinary Connection

Vayera can be read alongside safety-critical system design. Abraham's tent represents a generous default; Sodom represents rules that encode cruelty; Abraham's intercession functions like an adversarial audit of power; and “do not send your hand” functions like a hard stop that prevents irreversible harm.

The comparison clarifies a real principle. Good intentions do not make a system safe, because failure can arise from ordinary procedures, concentrated authority, or a mission that treats people as expendable. Evidence from Sodom, Hagar's wilderness, and the Akedah shows why ethical systems need monitoring, appeal, and abort conditions rather than trust in the operator's virtue alone.

The implication is governance, not reduction. Torah is not an engineering manual, and the analogy does not resolve the Akedah. It does expose a shared demand: any high-stakes mission must specify who can challenge it, whose distress counts as evidence, and where action must stop.

## Original Piyyut - At the Entrance of the Tent

**Status:** Original contemporary liturgical draft for responsive reading; it is not a traditional or inherited piyyut. Its phrases draw from Genesis 18-22.

**Leader:** At the entrance of the tent, in the heat of the day, teach us to lift our eyes from revelation and see the traveler waiting near us.

**Community:** *Vayar, vayaratz likratam.* He saw, and he ran to meet them.

**Leader:** When the cry rises from the city, do not let reverence become silence. Give us Abraham's question and the courage to ask whether power still serves justice.

**Community:** *Hashofet kol ha'aretz lo ya'aseh mishpat?* Shall the Judge of all the earth not do justice?

**Leader:** God who heard the boy in the wilderness, hear every child before fear names a future for them. Open our eyes to the well beside us.

**Community:** *Ba'asher hu sham.* Meet each soul where they are.

**Leader:** When devotion raises a hand against life, let Your interruption reach us in time. Give us the humility to stop, the wisdom to substitute, and the faith to bring the child home.

**Community:** *Al tishlach yadcha el hana'ar.* Do not send your hand against the child.

**Leader:** Make our homes open, our arguments honest, our promises careful, and our covenant a shelter for the living.

**Community:** At the entrance of the tent, may we see—and run toward mercy. Amen.
